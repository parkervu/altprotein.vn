# How Vietnamese publics react to fillers, blends and fake chay

Line: W9-L1 How Vietnamese publics react to fillers, blends and fake chay (revealed reaction) (wave 9). Source prefix: TRU. Date: 25 September 2026.

Tags: `[evidence label, confidence, source, demand evidence type]`. Press framing, enforcement and seller claims are tagged *revealed*: they show what outlets publish, what regulators act on and what sellers think buyers want. Quotes from consumers or experts are *stated*. "Our calculation" marks our arithmetic. Snippet-only evidence (a search-result extract, page not opened) is Low and says so.

## Verdict

The Vietnamese record from 2015 to 2026 splits cleanly. The word *độn* (padding, filler) is a fraud word: press advice, a provincial health centre, artisan sellers and consumers use it for hidden, cost-cutting starch in *giò* (pork roll) and *chả*, and some sellers now advertise "no soy protein filler". Yet we found no press story or enforcement action that treats *declared* soy protein in branded sausages as fraud, and Masan sells a "real meat stick" whose ingredient list includes soy protein and starch. Enforcement and scandal attach to borax, diseased meat, species substitution and untraceable goods, and 2026 is a year of acute processed-meat distrust. On the chay side we found no confirmed case of meat in a Vietnamese chay product: one unconfirmed 2021 suspicion, a relayed Taiwanese scandal and social-media videos. The live chay trust problems are borax, microbes and unlabelled origin. Yield class: **added a constraint** (the *độn* frame and a format constraint that moves TPP-03's first fine-emulsion test from *giò lụa* to industrial *xúc xích*), with two moves recast (DMV-07 re-weighted towards safety tests; DMV-14's value is defensive). It changes no number in ch28.

## Headline findings

1. **"Độn" is a fraud word for meat products, and the public test for good *giò lụa* is the absence of starch.** Advice columns from 2015 to 2020 (Dan Tri, VnExpress) and a provincial CDC page tell readers that *giò* without small air holes "has been mixed with starch and made with meat of poor quality" [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-02; TRU-29, revealed]. Retailers repeat the test in 2026 [VN-direct, Low, TRU-29, revealed].
2. **When pork prices spiked in 2019, sellers extended *giò* with starch and buyers treated it as cheating.** *Giò lụa* rose from VND 130,000 to 150,000 to 200,000 per kg (33 to 54% more, our calculation). A Hanoi shopper called the makers who added tapioca and corn starch "không đứng đắn" (dishonest) and made her own *giò*; a seller of 20 years said customers fell by nearly half [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-03, revealed and stated; the demand drop is one seller's account, Low]. This is the closest Vietnamese analogue to a cost-driven extender, and it was read as fraud.
3. **Artisan and online sellers compete on "no filler", and some name soy.** Sellers advertise *không độn bột* (no starch filler), *giò nguyên chất 100%* (pure) and, for *lạp xưởng* (Chinese-style sausage), *không độn đạm đậu nành, thịt gà công nghiệp* (no soy protein filler, no industrial chicken) [VN-direct, Low, TRU-27, revealed; snippets]. Additive suppliers sell recipes for *giò heo độn gà* (pork roll extended with chicken, with 10 g gluten per kg) and corn starch that "adds weight and lowers cost" [VN-direct, Low, TRU-28, revealed; snippets]. Extension is an open trade practice with a fraud name.
4. **Declared soy in branded processed meat draws no observed penalty.** In Dan Tri, 776 results for *xúc xích đậu nành* (sausage soy) and 548 for *đạm đậu nành* (soy protein) surfaced no story framing soy in processed meat as fraud; soy protein appears as a health food ("boneless meat"; an NIN and Vinasoy workshop recommending 25 g of soy a day) [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-22; TRU-23, revealed]. Masan's Omachi cup noodle is sold "with a real meat stick" (*có cây thịt thật*), a line advertised since at least 2018 (snippet); the stick lists meat, water, modified starch and *đạm đậu nành* (soy protein) [VN-direct, High, TRU-25, revealed]. This fits Part VI's point that 31% of Vissan meat formulations list soy (BUY-05).
5. **The processed-meat scandals that reach the press and the courts are about borax, disease and species, not plant protein.** Dan Tri reported at least six prosecutions of *giò chả* makers for borax (*hàn the*) between February and August 2026, after a Hanoi shop was found to have sold about 300 t of borax-treated product since 2022 [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-07; TRU-06, revealed]. About 300 t of pork from pigs with African swine fever reached school meals, and 120 to 130 t reached a canning plant in Hai Phong, in the first quarter of 2026 [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-20, revealed]. Pork was sold as beef in 2017 (beef balls labelled 70% or more beef, pork DNA only, criminal charges) and in 2025 and 2026 [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-09; TRU-12; NOV-72, revealed].
6. **Customers kept buying from the borax shop the next day.** A Dan Tri reporter counted 5 to 7 buyers in 20 minutes; regulars cited habit and taste [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-06, revealed]. Trust shocks hit categories and headlines more than a known local seller, at least in the short run.
7. **We found no confirmed case of meat in a Vietnamese chay product, 2015 to 2026.** The one official case is a seizure of more than 3 t of *chả đòn chay* (chay pork roll) in An Giang in April 2021, taken "on suspicion" (*nghi vấn*) of being mixed with meat and fish; we found no published result [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-18, revealed]. Chay enforcement otherwise concerns borax (6,573 kg seized in An Giang in 2022; tofu skin positive in Dong Nai in 2020), microbes and unlabelled origin [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-16; TRU-14; TRU-17; CHY-59; CHY-60, revealed]. The Can Tho University DNA kit paper cites "many cases" but tested no market samples [VN-direct, Medium, CHY-61, stated].
8. **Fake chay lives in relayed and social stories.** In 2015 VietNamNet reprinted a Taiwanese story (15 of 21 imitation-meat samples with animal ingredients) and warned that Vietnamese restaurants import Taiwanese mock meat [VN-adjacent, Low, TRU-01, revealed]. Facebook and provincial TV videos (about 2019 to 2026) claim meat or bone in *chả chay* [VN-direct, Low, TRU-30, revealed; snippets]. In 2026 the main chay exposé (Tien Phong) names additives, dyes and reused oil, not meat [VN-direct, Medium, CHY-23, revealed].
9. **A state lab already sells the meat-in-chay test.** QUATEST 3 announced on 4 February 2025 a real-time PCR screen for 86 vertebrate species at a relative detection limit of 0.001%, for halal, vegetarian food and feed, noting official and consumer concern about chay and origin fraud [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-24, revealed]. A 2017 forensic DNA test proved pork in "beef" balls (TRU-09).
10. **Positive framing exists when the plant part is a named, culinary or health ingredient.** Chicken-breast and shiitake sausages (*xúc xích ức gà nấm hương*) are sold as "healthy, eat clean", for example at VND 89,000 per 500 g (VND 178,000 per kg, our calculation) on a marketplace, and as a dish on a food-delivery app [VN-direct, Low, TRU-32, revealed]. Children's sausages name lotus seed or vegetables, and one value sausage names soy in its title (VND 126,000 per kg, our calculation) [VN-direct, Low, TRU-26, revealed]. We found no Vietnamese brand that markets a declared meat and plant-protein blend (clean negative) [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-22, revealed].
11. **The same outlet uses *thịt giả* (fake meat) for plant-based meat and for crime.** Of Dan Tri's first 11 results for *thịt giả*, 6 were about plant-based or cultivated meat and 4 about fraud; a 2020 headline told diners that soy pork in restaurants could be a *cú lừa* (a trick) [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-22; TRU-10, revealed].
12. **A 2016 composition survey of a traditional food backfired.** A consumer association reported that 101 of more than 150 fish-sauce samples (67.33%) exceeded arsenic limits; supermarkets pulled traditional fish sauce, the Ministry of Health said the arsenic was organic, and the press framed the survey as a "strike" on traditional makers [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-19, revealed]. This is the precedent a chay authenticity survey (DMV-07) must design around.

## 3. How soy already in processed meat is talked about (task 2)

- **Labels list soy and nobody reports it.** Vissan lists soy protein in 19 of 61 meat formulations (BUY-05); Masan's "real meat stick" lists *đạm đậu nành* (TRU-25); seven commercial emulsion sausages tested in 2018 all contained soy and were as well liked as alternatives (HXE-02). We found no complaint, exposé or test report on soy in these branded products [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-22, revealed].
- **Artisan sellers use soy as a negative marker.** The *lạp xưởng* claim "no soy protein filler, no industrial chicken" puts soy beside cheap chicken as something a good product lacks [VN-direct, Low, TRU-27, revealed; snippet]. Home sausage makers advertise "90% meat, no starch filler" [VN-direct, Low, TRU-27, revealed; snippet].
- **Suppliers see the problem.** One soy isolate supplier's page says plant protein is "overshadowed by the 'filler' label"; another sells soy for meat as a *chất độn* (filler), and one explains that 2% soy protein raises sausage weight by 10% [VN-direct, Low, TRU-28, revealed; snippets].
- **Advice columns target starch and borax, not soy.** Tests taught to readers are air holes, crumbliness, smell and turmeric paper for borax (TRU-02; TRU-05). We found no advice column that names soy protein.
- **Health media praise soy as a food.** Soy is "boneless meat" (2021), a "misunderstood" food (2026) and 25 g a day is recommended at a 2023 NIN and Vinasoy workshop (sponsored) [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-22; TRU-23, revealed].

Our reading [VN-direct, Medium, inferred]: soy is positive as a food and neutral as a listed ingredient in an industrial sausage. It turns negative when it is hidden, when it replaces meat to cut cost, or when it appears in an artisan product whose promise is purity.

## 4. Positive framing and open blends (task 3)

- **Named culinary ingredients sell.** Chicken-breast and shiitake sausages are a small "eat clean" category sold through neighbourhood Facebook groups, food apps and marketplaces (TRU-32). Traditional mixed products (*giò xào* with wood-ear mushroom, *chả cốm* with green rice, spring-roll fillings) are culinary norms. But in 2019 sellers raised the mushroom and skin share of *giò xào* to cut cost and the press listed that too as a cost-cutting trick (TRU-03).
- **Children's and value sausages name plant ingredients.** Xuxifarm sells a "healthy child" pork and lotus-seed sausage (VND 26,000 per 200 g) and a "braised beef and soy flavour" sausage (VND 25,000 per 198 g); another brand sells a "vegetable and cheese flavour" sausage [VN-direct, Low, TRU-26, revealed]. We could not read their ingredient lists.
- **Incumbents speak of eating less meat, but through chay.** In 2020 Vissan's deputy general director said that meat-eating families were turning to chay "to reduce protein from meat and fish and add fibre" [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-04, stated].
- **No declared blend.** We found no Vietnamese product sold as a blend of meat and plant protein, and Dan Tri has no story on one (0 results for "Meat Zero") [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-22, revealed].

## 6. What this means for TPP-03, TPP-02, R3, DMV-14 and DMV-07 (task 4)

**Would an openly declared 20 to 30% extender face a trust penalty?** It depends on the product, the channel and the words.
- *In industrial xúc xích, fillings and value lines*, a listed plant ingredient has no observed penalty: soy is already there, and Masan sells "real meat" with soy in it. The risk is a future exposé framing the extension as cost-cutting, which the 2026 climate makes more likely [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].
- *In giò lụa*, the public quality test is the absence of starch, artisan sellers compete on "no filler", and the one observed cost-driven extension (2019) cost sellers customers. A declared 20 to 30% extender here would be read as *giò độn* [VN-direct, Medium, inferred from TRU-02; TRU-03; TRU-27]. W8-L6 had already found that *giò lụa* tolerates only 10 to 20% in texture terms and that graininess cuts liking (HXE-01). Together these make *giò lụa* the worst first format.
- *Wording matters more than disclosure.* The penalty attaches to *độn* and to "meat replaced", not to an ingredient name. A label that names *nấm hương* (shiitake), *đạm nấm* (mushroom protein) or *hạt sen* (lotus seed) sits in a frame the market already rewards. A "30% plant protein" or "30% less meat" front-of-pack statement is untested and risks the *độn* reading. Informed tasting lowers demand for blends elsewhere (GLB-30) [general, Medium, tested].

**TPP-02 and DMV-07.** Fake chay is not a live issue. It would not make a protein claim less credible now. DMV-07 should put its money into the live problems (borax, preservatives, microbes, protein content) and treat animal DNA as a cheap screen bought from QUATEST 3. Its disclosure plan should learn from the 2016 fish-sauce survey: publish with the Vietnam Food Administration (VFA), publish methods, and do not name small traditional makers [VN-direct, Medium, TRU-19; TRU-24, inferred].

**DMV-14.** W8-L1 found that percentage labelling is already law when the meat is named. This line adds two things. First, declared percentages are enforced hard: the 2017 beef-ball maker who labelled 70% or more beef was charged criminally after a DNA test (TRU-09). Second, the fraud frame lives in unlabelled market *giò* and in small businesses exempt from nutrition labels, which a label rule does not reach. A voluntary processor code is therefore defensive: it is proof against a *độn* accusation, not a cure for fraud [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].

**R3 in ch28.** No number changes. The adoption basis should record that no Vietnamese brand sells a declared blend, that the public reads hidden extension as cheating, and that 2026 is a processed-meat trust crisis. D-BENCH (6% of processed meat as hybrids by 2035) is consistent with extension inside industrial lines under ingredient names. D-STRETCH (15% by 2035, 35% by 2050) assumes a positive blend frame that has not been observed in Vietnam [VN-direct, Low, inferred].

**Schools.** Hanoi parents took school-meal samples to the national food-safety testing institute in September 2026 to check meat composition (TRU-21). This supports W7-L1's rule that schools come after canteens and only as a named processor's labelled blend.

## Next-wave candidates

1. **Informed label test for a blended *xúc xích*** (different kind: tested, not desk). Three arms at equal price: no front claim, "có nấm hương / đạm nấm" (with mushroom protein), and "thịt heo 55%, thay 20% bằng đạm thực vật" (20% replaced by plant protein). About 300 shoppers in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi. Desk research cannot close it. If the "replaced" arm loses and the named-ingredient arm does not, the TPP-03 label rule stands; if both lose, R3 shrinks to undeclared binder use.
2. **State lab records of animal-DNA screens on chay** (different kind: records request). Ask QUATEST 3, NIFC and the An Giang authorities (for the 2021 case) what they have tested and found. Not desk-closable. A non-trivial positive rate would restore meat DNA to the front of DMV-07 and add "verified meat-free" as a real selling point for TPP-02.
3. **Social listening around pork price peaks** (deeper). Volume and sentiment of *độn* with *giò*, *chả*, *xúc xích* on Facebook and TikTok in 2019 to 2020 and 2026 through a data vendor. Not closable with our tools. It would size the trust penalty that a price-driven extender would face.
4. **Further press desk work on this question would be noise.** Two independent routes (Google and Dan Tri's own index) gave the same pattern; more searching would add citations, not change a recommendation.

## Limits

- Press coverage is uneven. Dan Tri is the only outlet we could search systematically; Tuoi Tre, Thanh Nien, VietNamNet and Suc khoe & Doi song were blocked to the fetch tool and Google stopped after a robot check. A clean negative from one index plus Google snippets is Medium at best.
- Many items are snippets (seller claims, supplier pages, advice columns, social posts). They show that a claim or phrase exists, not how common it is. Social reaction counts are platform displays, not reach.
- Dates for social posts are approximate ("7 years ago" read on 25 September 2026).
- The 2019 demand drop comes from one seller in one article, during a price spike that also cut demand by itself.
- We could not read ingredient lists for the Xuxifarm and other value sausages, so we cannot say whether soy in a product name marks a real soy content.
- We did not test consumers. Everything about how a declared blend would be received is inferred from reactions to undeclared fillers and from what sellers choose to claim.
