# Demand for the frontier categories in Vietnam: cultivated meat and seafood, precision fermentation, mycoprotein and blended products

Agent: NOVEL (wave 6). Source prefix: NOV. Date: 24 September 2026.

Part VI working paper, edition 1.2. Tags follow the brief: `[evidence label, confidence, source ID, demand evidence type]`. Supply-study context (retail audit, rules, incumbents) is cited, not re-researched. Existing IDs used: FORM-01, RD-32, ECO-24.

A note on method. The session's web search allowance ran out early. After that we worked from direct page fetches, OpenAlex and Scite, publisher files, and the site search of VnExpress, Thanh Nien, CafeF and Green Queen. Some items that a normal search would find (for example Vietnamese e-commerce listings for *chà bông nấm*, mushroom floss) were not reachable. Where that matters we say "we found no" and log it.

## Headline findings

1. **Vietnam has no tested or revealed evidence on cultivated meat, only stated attitudes from young or online samples.** A five-country Asian survey (n = 3,005) places Vietnamese respondents, with Chinese, in the most open segment ("Open-Minded Seekers") [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-01, stated]. A survey of 393 young Vietnamese finds only about a quarter are "optimists" about in vitro meat [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-03, stated]. Both are upper bounds. Asian studies that tested behaviour, not just attitudes, show lower acceptance than stated surveys do (finding 13).

2. **Revealed demand for cultivated meat in Asia is tiny after almost six years of legal sale in Singapore.** GOOD Meat reported "more than 2,000 servings" sold from December 2020 to May 2024 [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-10, revealed]. Vow reported "more than 25,000 dishes" from April 2024 to August 2025 [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-14, revealed]. Neither has published repeat purchase data. In November 2025 the Singapore Food Agency cited "weaker-than-expected consumer acceptance globally" and left cultivated meat out of its short-term food security plan [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-22, revealed].

3. **What has actually reached Asian customers is mostly plant or fat, with a small share of cells.** The only retail cultivated product in Asia, GOOD Meat 3, is 3% cultivated chicken, sold at S$7.20 (US$5.35) per 120 g [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-10, revealed]. Aleph Farms' steak, approved in Singapore in August 2026, is 10 to 20% cultivated beef in a soy and wheat matrix [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-18, revealed]. Vow sells fat-rich parfaits and foie gras [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-14, revealed]. The commercial form of cultivated meat is a hybrid ingredient, not a whole cut.

4. **We found no cultivated seafood approved for human food in Asia, but Vietnam's two largest seafood exporters bought early exposure.** Vinh Hoan took a stake in Avant Meats (January 2021) and co-invested in Shiok Meats (July 2021). Minh Phu signed an R&D memorandum with Shiok Meats (July 2022) [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-74, NOV-75, NOV-76, revealed]. This is strategic B2B interest, not consumer pull. Singapore's April 2026 list of 14 approved novel foods has no seafood [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-21, revealed].

5. **The "93% interested in blended products" figure measures interest in *trying*, among urban online respondents, and was commissioned by an advocacy group.** It comes from GFI APAC's *Decoding Demand* survey (n = 5,971, six Southeast Asian markets, with Ho Chi Minh City as the Vietnam sample). The same survey found only 5% eat plant-based meat regularly [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-35, NOV-36, ECO-24, stated]. The better evidence comes from a blind taste test in Singapore. There, blends were 1.8 times more likely than plant-based meat to be liked, and purchase intent for blends was 1.4 to 1.5 times that for plant-based meat. Both stayed below conventional meat. The preferred mix was about 75% animal [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-37, NOV-39, NOV-40, tested].

6. **Vietnam already eats blends without a label penalty, and this is the most likely entry route.** Vissan's mainstream sausage is 58% lean pork plus soy protein, starch and fibre [VN-direct, High, NOV-56, revealed]. Its *giò lụa* (pork roll) lists both soy and milk protein [VN-direct, High, NOV-57, revealed]. TH true NUT walnut "nut milk" contains fresh cow's milk and 1.0 g protein per 100 ml [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-58, revealed]. A fungal or fermented protein that replaces 20 to 30% of the meat in these formats, sold B2B to processors, fits an existing slot. A novel analogue sold to consumers does not [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].

7. **"Artificial" and "fake" are loaded words in Vietnam right now.** The press term for cultivated meat is mostly *thịt nhân tạo* (artificial meat). The same term covers plant-based meat, and even cultivated shrimp is called *thịt tôm nhân tạo* [VN-direct, High, NOV-69, NOV-74, inferred]. A leading financial outlet framed cultivated meat as a "nightmare" using cells "that may cause cancer" [VN-direct, High, NOV-71, inferred]. In 2025 and 2026 Vietnam had fake-egg rumours that hurt egg producers and a 65-tonne fake-beef bust in Ho Chi Minh City [VN-direct, High, NOV-72, NOV-73, inferred]. We found no Vietnamese test of names.

8. **A GMO label is a strong negative for Vietnamese consumers, which matters because precision-fermented proteins probably count as GMO products under Decree 43/2026 (supply study).** An experimental auction found an 84% willingness-to-pay premium for non-GM soymilk [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-08, tested]. VitaDairy and Vinasoy advertise *không biến đổi gen* (non-GMO) as a benefit [VN-direct, High, NOV-62, NOV-63, revealed]. GM maize has been grown since 2015, on 10.2% of maize area in 2019 [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-09, revealed]. Acceptance of a feed crop tells us little about acceptance of a labelled food.

9. **"Fermented" (*lên men*) is a positive word in Vietnamese food marketing and media.** "Genetically modified" is a negative one, and precision fermentation sits between the two. Vinasoy sells a "naturally fermented" nut milk on digestive-health claims [VN-direct, High, NOV-63, revealed]. VnExpress frames fermented foods as good for gut health first, with intolerance caveats [VN-direct, High, NOV-64, inferred]. 46% of young urban Vietnamese said they would try an energy bar made with brewer's yeast protein [VN-direct, Medium, RD-32, stated]. Nobody has tested *lên men* against other labels on a protein product.

10. **Demand for precision-fermented proteins in Asia is B2B and high-value, and Vietnamese formula makers already buy the target ingredient.** Examples: lactoferrin (conventional price about US$800 per kg, per one company CEO) [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-45, revealed]; whey protein (beta-lactoglobulin) for sports and medical nutrition, which Perfect Day says is sold out [general, Low, NOV-44, revealed]; and sweet proteins (Amai approved in Singapore in May 2026; Bestzyme's 100-tonne line in China) [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-47, NOV-48, revealed]. VitaDairy, Nutifood and Morinaga all market lactoferrin formulas in Vietnam in 2025 and 2026 [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-59, NOV-60, NOV-61, revealed]. We found no Vietnamese company that has shown public interest in precision-fermented versions. The same companies market GMO-free claims.

11. **Mycoprotein has not yet proved itself with Asian consumers.** Quorn's owner, the Philippine firm Monde Nissin, saw meat-alternative sales fall from about PHP 15.4 billion (2022) to PHP 13.6 billion (2024). 78.6% of 2024 sales were in the UK, and Monde Nissin publishes no Asian breakdown [general, High, NOV-50, revealed]. China approved its first mycoprotein in December 2025, as a B2B ingredient [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-49, revealed]. Vietnam's mushroom habit is a bridge, but Vietnamese readers criticise mushroom mock meat for heavy seasoning and additives [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-65, stated]. A clean label is a design requirement, not a bonus.

12. **Price is the largest single barrier in Vietnamese data.** In the Hanoi study, price had the largest coefficient on attitude (-0.359) and a significant negative effect on willingness to try (-0.250) [VN-direct, High, NOV-02, stated]. At Singapore prices, GOOD Meat 3 costs about VND 405,000 per 100 g of protein. That is close to the imported analogues in the retail audit (VND 542,000 to 622,000) and 9 to 12 times the cost of chicken breast or eggs (VND 34,000 to 46,000) [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-10, FORM-01, inferred].

13. **In Asian studies that tested behaviour, acceptance was lower than the stated figures, so Vietnamese stated openness should be treated as a ceiling.** In an online survey published in 2019, 59.3% of an urban-skewed Chinese sample said they were very or extremely likely to buy "purity meat" [general, High, NOV-28, stated]. In a 2020 choice experiment, Chinese consumers would pay less for cultivated than for farm-raised meat [general, Medium, NOV-29, tested]. Japan's social acceptance of cultured meat was 40.9%, against 70.2% for plant-based meat [general, Medium, NOV-30, stated]. In a Singapore cohort (n = 1,224), perceived unnaturalness was the strongest barrier to cultivated meat, and distrust of gene technology was a barrier specific to it [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-31, stated].

14. **Religious rules are workable for halal export but uncertain for Vietnam's *chay* (Buddhist vegetarian) market.** Singapore's Islamic council (2024) and Malaysia's JAKIM (December 2025) accept cultivated meat as halal under conditions: halal-slaughtered donor cells, halal media and no fetal bovine serum [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-52, NOV-53, revealed]. We found no Vietnamese Buddhist ruling. Some Vietnamese readers doubt that even plant mock meat is truly vegetarian "in the mind" [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-65, stated]. Cultivated meat is unlikely to be treated as *chay* [VN-direct, Low, inferred].

## 1. Cultivated meat and seafood: what Vietnamese evidence exists

### 1.1 Academic and survey evidence

We searched OpenAlex and Scite in English, and Vietnamese press sites in Vietnamese: *thịt nuôi cấy* (cultured meat), *thịt nhân tạo* (artificial meat) and *thịt trong phòng thí nghiệm* (laboratory meat). Only two studies give Vietnamese data on cultivated meat itself. Two more cover "novel alternative proteins" in general.

| Study | Sample and frame | Finding | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lin et al. 2026, five Asian countries | n = 3,005 across Singapore, Vietnam, China, Japan, South Korea; panel details and country split not retrieved | Three segments. "Open-Minded Seekers" (high value, low neophobia, urban, educated) are concentrated in Vietnam and China. Japan dominates "Resistant Evaluators". | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-01, stated] |
| Nghiem-Phu 2023 | n = 393 young Vietnamese | Pessimists, optimists, mainstreamers; optimists about a quarter | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-03, stated] |
| Nguyen M.Q. et al. 2025 (HUST) | n = 288, aged 18 to 25, Hanoi | Novel proteins (plant-based meat, yeast protein, insects): 70% aware but never used (text says 84%); price the largest negative factor | [VN-direct, High, NOV-02, stated] |
| Nguyen M.Q. et al. 2024 (HUST) | n = 260, aged 18 to 25, Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City | 46% willing to try a brewer's yeast protein bar | [VN-direct, Medium, RD-32, stated] |

What this does not tell us. No Vietnamese study has measured behaviour. None used a representative sample or tested a name. None measured willingness to pay a stated price, and the HUST studies covered only people aged 18 to 25 in the two big cities. The Lin et al. country table is the most useful missing piece. We could read only the abstract, and we have logged it as an open question for the authors.

We found no GFI APAC survey with Vietnamese results on cultivated meat. *Decoding Demand* covered plant-based and blended meat, not cultivated meat [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-35]. We found no Bryant-style multi-country cultivated meat study that included Vietnam. The ten-country Siegrist and Hartmann internet survey (n = 6,128) does not include Vietnam [general, Medium, NOV-82, stated], and nor does Bryant's USA, India and China survey [general, High, NOV-28, stated].

### 1.2 Vietnamese media framing

We read 14 Vietnamese articles from 2019 to 2026 (register in `data_vn_media_framing.csv`). Four points stand out.

- **Coverage is foreign news.** Every cultivated meat story except one reports approvals, bans or company news abroad and quotes US survey data. The exception is a 2022 VnExpress piece on preliminary stem-cell meat research at the University of Science, VNU Ho Chi Minh City [VN-direct, High, NOV-66, NOV-67, NOV-68, NOV-70, inferred].
- **There is no settled name.** One Thanh Nien article uses *thịt nuôi cấy*, *thịt nhân tạo* and *thịt phòng thí nghiệm* in the same text [VN-direct, High, NOV-81, inferred]. The government newspaper used *thịt nhân tạo* for Beyond Meat style plant-based meat [VN-direct, High, NOV-69, inferred]. CafeF called Shiok Meats' cultivated shrimp *thịt tôm nhân tạo* (artificial shrimp meat) [VN-direct, High, NOV-74, inferred]. Vietnamese consumers therefore meet "artificial meat" as one blurred category.
- **Tone is mostly neutral or curious, with one prominent fear frame.** CafeF (December 2023) headlined the "nightmare" of cultivated meat firms, tiny sales and "cells that may cause cancer", relaying Bloomberg [VN-direct, High, NOV-71, inferred]. We treat the cancer line as a framing, not a finding. The article gives no safety evidence.
- **The wider food-fraud climate is hostile to anything that "imitates" meat.** In May 2025, social-media rumours of fake eggs hurt poultry farmers, and the Vietnam Poultry Association asked police to investigate [VN-direct, High, NOV-73, inferred]. In May 2026 Ho Chi Minh City police broke a ring that had made 65 tonnes of "fake beef" from chemically treated pork since 2024 [VN-direct, High, NOV-72, inferred]. *Thịt giả* (fake meat) is a crime story in Vietnam. A product marketed as "just like real meat" invites that association [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].

We found no evidence that *thịt nhân tạo* is used as a deliberate scare term. But the word means "man-made" and sits close to "fake" in the current news climate. That makes it a poor name for a product, and the cause of the damage would not matter.

Three patterns matter for Vietnam.

First, **volumes are symbolic**. Two thousand servings in three and a half years, and 25,000 dishes in 16 months, are trial, novelty and chef-led volumes. They are not household demand [VN-adjacent, Medium, inferred]. For comparison, the only US figure we saw was relayed second-hand: Upside Foods reportedly sold about one pound a month in 2023 [general, Low, NOV-71, revealed].

Second, **nobody publishes repeat purchase**. The only "repeat" measure is a company-reported score of 4.41 out of 5 for willingness to retry among Huber's Bistro diners [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-10, stated]. That is stated intent from self-selected triers.

Third, **the buyers are premium restaurants and curious urban diners**, then pet owners. Nothing suggests mass-market or institutional uptake. Singapore's own regulator now describes global consumer acceptance as weaker than expected [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-22, revealed].

### 2.3 Cultivated seafood

- We found no cultivated seafood sold for human food in Asia. Umami Bioworks, which absorbed Shiok Meats, said in September 2025 that its human-food dossier was in the "final stage" in Singapore. It launched pet food first [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-24, revealed]. The April 2026 Singapore list contains no seafood [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-21, revealed].
- Vietnamese seafood incumbents showed strategic interest early. Vinh Hoan's CEO said Avant Meats offered "a unique opportunity ... to diversify our future product portfolio" [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-75, revealed]. Minh Phu agreed to explore a joint cultivated shrimp R&D facility in Vietnam [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-76, revealed]. We found no later news on either tie. This is investor and R&D demand for options, not buyer demand for product.

The pattern is clear. Consumer attitude data are thin. In Singapore, 56.2% said they would probably or definitely try a precision-fermented egg and 48.1% would buy it. Price was the top reason to buy (17.1%) [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-34, stated]. That study was funded by a precision-fermentation company.

### 3.2 Vietnamese buyers

- **Lactoferrin is already a marketing ingredient in Vietnam.** VitaDairy launched ColosBaby Lactoferrin 3+ in 2026 with sponsored coverage in VnExpress [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-59, revealed]. Nutifood sells GrowPLUS+ Colostrum Lactoferrin [VN-direct, Low, NOV-61, revealed]. Morinaga promotes its lactoferrin factory in Vietnamese media [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-60, revealed]. The buyers exist, and they pay for the ingredient.
- **The same buyers sell GMO-free.** VitaDairy's Moozi milk lists "không biến đổi gen" among its "10 KHÔNG" (10 NOs), next to hormones and antibiotic residues [VN-direct, High, NOV-62, revealed]. If a precision-fermented lactoferrin must be declared as GMO, it collides with the brand promise of its most likely Vietnamese buyers [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].
- **We found no public statement of interest in precision fermentation from Vinamilk, TH, Nutifood or VitaDairy.** Site searches of VnExpress, Thanh Nien and CafeF for *lên men chính xác* (precision fermentation) returned no articles [VN-direct, Medium, revealed]. This is a limited search, not proof of absence.

## 4. GMO and "biotech food" attitudes; the meaning of fermentation

### 4.1 GM food in Vietnam

| Evidence | Tag |
|---|---|
| Experimental auction: 84% premium for soymilk with non-GM information; risk aversion the main driver | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-08, tested] |
| Online survey, n = 424: knowledge raises perceived benefits and lowers risks; trust works through perceived benefits (indirect effect 0.234) | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-04, stated] |
| Generation Z, n = 416: knowledge acts through perceived benefits; risk pathway not supported | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-05, stated] |
| Implicit association test, n = 407: implicit attitudes to genome-edited and GM foods are highly correlated unless in-depth information is given | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-07, tested] |
| Genome-edited rice with consumer traits: wide acceptance, higher with in-depth information | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-06, stated] |
| GM maize commercial since 2015; 92,000 ha (10.2% of maize area) in 2019 | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-09, revealed] |

Read together, these say three things. Vietnamese consumers will pay a lot to avoid a "GM" food. They lump new biotech together with GMO unless given detail. Explanation helps, but explanation is costly at the shelf [VN-direct, Medium, inferred]. The Singapore cohort adds that distrust of gene technology is a barrier specific to cultivated meat [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-31, stated].

On trust in regulators, we found no Vietnamese survey that measures trust in the food regulator itself. The pork value chain study (n = 170) found low trust in value chain actors, government named as responsible for safety, and higher trust in government TV news [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-79, stated].

### 4.2 Does *lên men* help?

Vietnam's food culture is built on fermentation: *nước mắm* (fish sauce), *tương* (fermented soybean paste), *mắm* (fermented fish or shrimp), *dưa* (pickled vegetables) and *men* (starter cultures). We found revealed marketing evidence that *lên men* (fermented) sells:

- Vinasoy's Veyo is promoted as "*sữa hạt lên men tự nhiên*" (naturally fermented nut milk), with "better digestion and health from within" [VN-direct, High, NOV-63, revealed]. This is sponsored content, which shows the frame an incumbent chose.
- VnExpress opens a 2026 health piece by calling fermented foods "delicious" and rich in "beneficial bacteria for gut health", before listing intolerance symptoms [VN-direct, High, NOV-64, inferred].
- Young consumers stated moderate willingness (46%) to try a protein bar from brewer's yeast. Their concerns were flavour, safety and price [VN-direct, Medium, RD-32, stated].

We found no study that tests *lên men* against *vi sinh* (microbial), *nấm men* (yeast) or *công nghệ sinh học* (biotechnology) on a protein label. Our inference: "fermented" is the best available frame for biomass fermentation products like yeast and fungal protein [VN-direct, Medium, inferred]. For precision fermentation, the frame is weaker, because the mandatory description may be GMO whatever the front-of-pack word [VN-direct, Low, inferred].

## 5. Mycoprotein and fungal foods

- **Quorn in Asia.** Monde Nissin's 2024 filing shows the meat-alternative business at 20.8%, 17.7% and 16.4% of group sales in 2022, 2023 and 2024. Applied to group sales, that is about PHP 15.4, 14.2 and 13.6 billion (our calculation). The segment reported net losses of PHP 20.4, 9.8 and 10.4 billion, against core losses of PHP 0.3, 1.0 and 0.8 billion. 78.6% of 2024 segment sales came from the UK, 5.6% from the US and 15.8% from the rest of the world, with no Asian split [general, High, NOV-50, revealed]. H1 2026 revenue rose 2.2% at constant currency, helped by a new snacking line in the UK [general, Medium, NOV-51, revealed]. A Philippine owner has not made mycoprotein an Asian consumer success.
- **China** approved Fushine Bio's Fusarium mycoprotein in December 2025 as a B2B ingredient. It has 1,200 t per year of capacity now and plans for 200,000 t [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-49, revealed]. Singapore has approved The Better Meat Co's Rhiza mycoprotein and The Protein Brewery's Fermotein [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-21, revealed].
- **Vietnam.** Mushrooms are already part of *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food) and of daily cooking; *nấm đùi gà* (king oyster mushroom) is a common meat-texture substitute. We could not retrieve e-commerce or sales data for mushroom floss, mushroom jerky or Emmay. Emmay claims 3 to 5 additives against 15 to 23 for other brands [VN-direct, Low, NOV-77, stated]. That positioning matches the main complaint we found: a VnExpress reader piece objecting that mushroom mock meat is "four spoons of seasoning to 100 g of mushroom", and that eating imitation meat is not truly vegetarian in spirit [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-65, stated]. National production figures disagree tenfold, 250,000 t against 25,000 t [VN-direct, Low, NOV-78] (see `disagreements.csv`).

**Implication.** The fungal bridge is real in cuisine. But the product that crosses it has to be short-label and not obviously "fake", and probably sold inside familiar dishes rather than as a standalone meat analogue [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].

## 6. Blended (hybrid) products

### 6.1 What the 93% figure is

GFI APAC commissioned *Decoding Demand* from The Good Growth Co. and published it in early 2024. It was an online survey of 5,971 respondents in capital-city regions of Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand, plus Ho Chi Minh City, with focus groups of eight per country [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-35, NOV-36, stated]. GFI's press release says "93 percent of surveyed consumers expressed interest in at least trying blended meat". It also says over 75% of sceptics and 80% of those who rejected plant-based meat were interested in blends [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-35, stated]. The full report is form-gated. We did not see the question wording, the Vietnam result or the fieldwork dates. The same survey found 5% eat plant-based meat regularly and 80% would buy it at 20% below meat prices [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-35, NOV-36, stated]. Our reading: 93% is a measure of low resistance to the idea, not of demand.

### 6.2 Tested evidence on blends

In November 2025, A*STAR's food institute (SIFBI) ran a double-blind test for GFI APAC and NECTAR with more than 100 tasters (116 per one report) and 20 blended products in 10 categories [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-37, NOV-40, tested]:

- A chicken mince with 30 to 50% plant protein beat 100% chicken. Several blends came within 0.5 points of meat on a 7-point scale [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-37, tested].
- Blends were 1.8 times more likely than plant-based meat to be liked. On average, animal meat was still liked by more tasters: 60% against 37% in one report, 81% against 59% (and 44% for plant-based) in another [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-38, NOV-39, NOV-40, tested].
- Purchase intent was 22% against 16% (plant-based) in one report and 58% against 38% (plant-based) and 95% (conventional) in another. The ratio is consistent [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-38, NOV-39, tested].
- 57% preferred blends of about 75% animal meat; 18% preferred 50:50. Nuggets were the favourite format (64%) [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-40, tested].
- 69% rated blends healthier than conventional meat [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-38, NOV-40, stated].

Other Asian precedents: Japan's Ginga Foods sells a 50:50 pork and plant sausage to restaurants, hotels, school lunches and canteens, not to retail [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-41, revealed]. Changing Bio in China combines yeast protein with dairy or plant ingredients for bakery buyers [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-42, revealed]. The cultivated meat products that reached Asian shelves are themselves blends (section 2.1). In a blind test in Israel (n = 60, company-commissioned), Aleph's cells-plus-plant steak matched beef on acceptance (96% against 98%) and purchase intent (50% against 51%) [general, Low, NOV-19, tested].

### 6.3 Vietnam's existing blends

| Product | Blend | Price | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vissan Xuc xich Dinh Duong Heo (pork sausage) | Lean pork 58%, pork fat, soy protein, starch, fibre, DHA | VND 20,500 per 175 g (about VND 117,000 per kg) | [VN-direct, High, NOV-56, revealed] |
| Vissan beef version | Lean beef 58%, pork fat, soy protein, starch | VND 19,500 to 20,500 per 175 g | [VN-direct, High, NOV-56, revealed] |
| Vissan *giò lụa* 500 g | Pork, pork fat, milk protein, soy protein | VND 103,100 (about VND 206,000 per kg) | [VN-direct, High, NOV-57, revealed] |
| TH true NUT walnut | Water, fresh cow's milk, walnut; 1.0 g protein per 100 ml | VND 48,500 (pack size unclear) | [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-58, revealed] |

Soy and milk proteins in these meats are functional binders at undisclosed inclusion rates. They are not declared replacements. Still, three facts follow. Vietnamese shoppers already buy mixed-protein meat products from the country's largest processors. The ingredient list carries no stigma serious enough to stop mass sales. And the price point (about VND 117,000 to 206,000 per kg) is what a fungal or fermented extender must help, not hurt [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].

### 6.4 Is blending the most likely entry route?

Yes, for fungal, biomass-fermented and plant proteins, and in the medium term for cultivated cells as a flavour or fat ingredient [VN-direct, Medium, inferred]. The evidence lines up:

- It avoids the "fake meat" frame, because the product is still meat, and the "artificial" frame, because the novel part is an ingredient [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].
- It matches revealed Asian practice: cultivated products are 3 to 20% cells, and blends go B2B to canteens and processors [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-10, NOV-18, NOV-41].
- It tolerates price. A 20 to 30% extender only has to beat the cost of lean meat per kg, not replace all of it [VN-direct, Low, inferred].
- The tested evidence favours blends over plant-based analogues on liking and intent [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-37, NOV-39].

What blending does not guarantee: a buyer. We found no Vietnamese processor statement of interest in fungal or fermented extenders, and no tasting data from Vietnam (open questions).

## 7. Naming, trust and religion

### 7.1 Names

- In Singapore, "cultivated meat" was the preferred name and was linked to more positive attitudes; "cell-based" was least liked. Animal welfare ("reduces animal slaughter") and emissions frames increased acceptance among Buddhists [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-32, NOV-33, stated].
- GFI's 2026 naming research (n = 3,500, apparently not Asian) found "lab-grown" the most recognised term. Process descriptions ("grown from cells", "grown outside the animal") were the most polarising, and benefit phrases had the highest net appeal [general, Low, NOV-80, stated].
- In China, a study published in 2019 chose the Chinese name "纯净肉" (purity meat) after pre-testing [general, High, NOV-28, stated]. This reminds us that high stated Chinese acceptance was measured under a flattering name.
- In Vietnamese, *thịt nhân tạo* (artificial meat) dominates the press and *thịt nuôi cấy* (cultured meat) appears from 2023. We did not see *thịt tế bào* (cell meat) in the articles we read [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-67, NOV-68, NOV-81, inferred]. No name has been tested with Vietnamese consumers. The ten-country evidence suggests that process-heavy names and descriptions raise feelings of unnaturalness and disgust [general, Medium, NOV-82, NOV-80, stated].

### 7.2 Halal

| Authority | Ruling | Conditions | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| MUIS, Singapore, 2024 | Permissible | Donor cells from halal-slaughtered animals; growth media free of *najis* or *haram* components; production preserves ritual purity and avoids cross-contamination | [VN-adjacent, High, NOV-52, revealed] |
| JAKIM National Muzakarah Committee, Malaysia, December 2025 | Can be halal | Halal donor animal (not pig); halal culture medium; no fetal bovine serum; all growth factors, hormones, enzymes, gelatin halal | [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-53, revealed] |
| Muslim consumers, Singapore (n = 658) | Halal consciousness shapes attention to benefit messages | | [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-54, stated] |

We found no ruling from Indonesia's authorities. For Vietnam, halal matters for exporting ingredients to Malaysia, Indonesia and the Gulf more than for the domestic market [VN-adjacent, Low, inferred].

### 7.3 Is cultivated meat *chay*?

We found no Vietnamese Buddhist Sangha position on cultivated meat or precision-fermented dairy. The scholarly comment by Korean Buddhist scholars in *Nature Food* (2023) was paywalled and not read [general, Low, NOV-55]. Two pieces of evidence point the same way. The Singapore study shows Buddhists respond to "less slaughter" framing [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-33, stated]. But a Vietnamese reader debate on mock meat argues that eating imitation meat "with a meat-eating mind" is not truly vegetarian [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-65, stated]. Products made from animal cells are very unlikely to be sold as *chay* in Vietnam. Precision-fermented dairy proteins made without animals are an open question [VN-direct, Low, inferred]. Frontier firms should not plan on the *chay* market for cell-based products.

## 8. Implications for frontier actors

**What to make.** For the next five years, the Vietnamese demand evidence supports **ingredients for blends** over finished novel products:

- Fungal (mycelium or mushroom), biomass-fermented or plant proteins that replace **20 to 30% (up to 50%) of meat** in formats Vietnamese people already buy: *xúc xích* (sausage), *giò lụa* and *chả lụa* (pork rolls), dumpling and spring roll fillings, meatballs, nuggets and *chà bông* (floss).
- The finished product should keep at least the protein of the meat product it modifies (aim for 10 g or more per 100 g; the retail audit found only 16% of ready-to-eat or ready-to-cook alternative foods reach 10 g, FORM-01) and cost the processor less per kg than the lean meat it replaces [VN-direct, Medium, FORM-01, NOV-56, inferred].
- Sensory target: the best Singapore blends matched or beat meat at 30 to 50% plant replacement, and most tasters preferred about 75% animal [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-37, NOV-40, tested].

**Price.** In Vietnamese stated data, price outweighs health, environment and novelty [VN-direct, High, NOV-02, stated]. Consumer-facing novel products at Singapore-style prices (about VND 405,000 per 100 g protein) would sit in the price band of the imported analogues, a category the retail audit found almost absent from shelves [VN-adjacent, Low, NOV-10, FORM-01, inferred].

**Channel.** The first buyers are firms, not households: processors (Vissan, CJ Foods Vietnam, SG Food, Cholimex), institutional canteens and school or factory meal providers, and infant, medical and sports nutrition makers for precision-fermented ingredients [VN-direct, Medium, inferred]. Asian precedents show the same order: Japan's 50:50 sausage goes to canteens and hotels; mycoprotein and sweet proteins in China go B2B [VN-adjacent, Medium, NOV-41, NOV-49, NOV-48].

**Label and name.** Avoid *nhân tạo* (artificial) and anything that sounds like *giả* (fake). Avoid "100% like real meat" claims in a market with live food-fraud stories [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-72, NOV-73, inferred]. Lead with *lên men* (fermented) for biomass fermentation, with *đạm nấm* (mushroom protein) or the mushroom species for fungal ingredients, and with benefit claims: less fat, no antibiotic residues, digestibility [VN-direct, Low, NOV-63, NOV-79, inferred]. Test names before launch (open questions).

By actor:

- **Founders and startups.** Sell to processors and canteens first. Publish protein per 100 g and price per kg against lean pork. Build a short, clean label (the main complaint about mushroom mock meat is additives). For precision fermentation, target lactoferrin or whey for export or B2B nutrition, and settle the GMO labelling question before any Vietnamese consumer product. Cultivated meat or seafood has no domestic consumer demand to design for before the mid-2030s. Treat Vietnam as an R&D, seafood partner or export base, and design products as hybrid ingredients [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].
- **R&D bodies and universities.** Close the tested-evidence gap cheaply. Run a blind central-location test of a 20 to 30% fungal or fermented blend in *giò lụa* or *xúc xích* (Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City), a randomised name and label test (*thịt nuôi cấy*, *thịt tế bào*, *thịt nhân tạo*; *lên men*, *vi sinh*, *biến đổi gen*), and a price choice experiment. HUST's group behind RD-32 and NOV-02 is the obvious partner.
- **Investors and development finance.** Discount stated acceptance. Asian revealed sales of cultivated meat are in the thousands of servings and Singapore has deprioritised it. Look for offtake letters from processors or nutrition makers, not survey percentages. Vinh Hoan and Minh Phu are the domestic strategic co-investors to approach for seafood [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-74, NOV-76, inferred].
- **Funders and philanthropies.** Fund public-good evidence: the Vietnam breakout of regional surveys, the naming test, a blend tasting study and a lactoferrin and processed-meat market sizing. These are cheap and decide product design.
- **Policy makers.** The new Food Safety Law (expected October to November 2026) could (a) set neutral names that avoid *nhân tạo*, (b) clarify whether highly purified precision-fermented proteins need a GMO declaration, (c) require honest percentage labelling of blends, so blends are not confused with fraud, and (d) open a novel-food route. Each point has a demand-side reason documented above [VN-direct, Medium, inferred].
- **Food and feed manufacturers.** Blending is not new to you: soy and milk proteins are already in your sausages and pork rolls. Pilot a mushroom or mycelium extender at 20 to 30% in one existing SKU, measure cost, protein and repeat sales, and publish nothing that implies imitation.
- **International organisations.** Support a representative (not online-only) national survey on novel foods, including rural and older consumers. All current Vietnamese data are urban and young.

**What not to do.** Do not launch premium consumer cultivated meat in Vietnam. Do not use the 93% figure as a market size. Do not name products *thịt nhân tạo*. Do not market cultivated or precision-fermented products to *chay* consumers. Do not put GMO forward as a selling point. Do not size a market from stated willingness to try.

**How large could it be?** We cannot size the blend route yet. We found no figure for Vietnam's processed meat volume (open question). The supply study's S-ALT scenario assumes 1% of meat demand is replaced by 2030. Blends inside processed meats are the most plausible vehicle for reaching that 1%, but this is our inference, not a measurement [VN-direct, Low, inferred].

## 9. What would change this view

- A Vietnamese blind tasting or market trial showing that consumers accept, and processors adopt, a fungal or fermented blend at price parity would move blending from "most likely" to "demonstrated". A clear rejection would push us towards plant-based *chay* improvements only.
- Published repeat purchase data from Singapore (GOOD Meat 3, Vow, Aleph from 2027) showing sustained household or restaurant demand would raise the case for cultivated products. Continued silence, or more staff cuts, would lower it further.
- A regulatory decision that purified precision-fermented proteins do not need a GMO label on consumer products would open direct-to-consumer routes for precision-fermented dairy and egg. A decision that they do would confine them to B2B and export.
- A Vietnamese Buddhist Sangha statement accepting precision-fermented dairy or cultivated meat as *chay* would open the largest existing substitute market.
- The Vietnam country results in Lin et al. 2026 or *Decoding Demand* could show much higher, or lower, openness than the regional averages imply.
- A named Vietnamese processor or formula maker publicly sourcing fungal, fermented or precision-fermented proteins would be the first revealed domestic B2B demand.
