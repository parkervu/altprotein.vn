# CLM: Climate and environment drivers of Vietnam's protein supply, 2030 to 2050

Stream: wave 3 (futures), agent CLM. Question tree: F1 (drivers to 2050) and F3 (inputs that change). Source prefix: CLM. All sources accessed 2026-09-24. Edition 1.0 source IDs (MAC, COST, REG, GT) are cited where we build on them.

## Headline findings

1. **Agriculture faces steep official caps.** The National Climate Change Strategy (Decision 896/QD-TTg, 2022) caps agricultural emissions at 64 MtCO2e in 2030 and 56 MtCO2e in 2050. Against the 104.5 MtCO2e emitted in 2020, that is 39% and 46% lower (our calculation). [VN-direct] High; projection (official target); 2030, 2050. CLM-01, CLM-08.
2. **Livestock methane is capped at 15.2 MtCO2e in 2030; rice at 30.7 MtCO2e.** (Decision 942/QD-TTg, methane plan.) By our estimate the livestock cap is about 30% below 2020 and 40% below 2030 business-as-usual. With the 2030 meat target of 6.0 to 6.5 Mt, methane per tonne of meat would need to fall by roughly a third in five years, mainly through manure and biogas measures. [VN-direct] High for caps, Low for our estimate; projection and estimate; 2030. CLM-02, CLM-34, REG-54.
3. **Rice, not livestock, is the main agricultural methane lever.** Rice is two-thirds of the agricultural methane cap (30.7 of 45.9 MtCO2e). The climate case for alternative protein in Vietnam therefore rests on imported-feed footprint, manure and land, not on national methane totals. This confirms edition 1.0. [VN-direct] High; trend; 2030. CLM-02, MAC-33.
4. **NDC 3.0 (2026 to 2035) is not confirmed as submitted.** It was in draft in January 2026, with the Prime Minister asking for approval in Q1 2026. A reported draft caps energy emissions at 380 to 519 MtCO2e in 2035. We found no agriculture caps for 2035 and no entry in the UNFCCC registry pages we could read. [VN-direct] Medium; signal; 2035. CLM-03 to CLM-07, CLM-42.
5. **In the Mekong Delta, groundwater policy matters more than global emissions to 2050.** The delta sits on average only about 0.8 m above sea level. Groundwater pumping sinks it about 1.1 cm a year, roughly ten times global sea-level rise. From 2018 to 2100, subsidence from pumping ranges from 12 cm to 100 cm depending on extraction policy. IPCC AR6 puts global sea-level rise at 0.44 to 0.76 m by 2100 under SSP2-4.5. [VN-direct] High; trend and projection; 2050, 2100. CLM-12, CLM-13, CLM-14, CLM-28.
6. **Salinity expands mostly for human reasons.** By around 2050, subsidence and riverbed incision from sand mining and trapped sediment could enlarge the salinity-affected area by 10 to 27%. Sea-level rise adds another 6 to 19%. [VN-direct] Medium; projection; 2050. CLM-15.
7. **Adaptation is already cutting crop losses.** The 2019 to 2020 dry season was the worst salinity event on record: 1.69 million ha (42.5% of the delta) affected. Yet only 26,000 ha of rice was lost outright. In 2024, about 1,580 ha of rice was at risk. [VN-direct] Medium; trend and signal; observed 2020 and 2024. CLM-36, CLM-37.
8. **Dangerous humid heat rises fastest in the Mekong Delta.** Under SSP2-4.5, annual maximum WBGT (a heat-stress index) rises about 0.3 °C per decade across Vietnam; the rate is 0.4 °C under SSP5-8.5. Before 2040 the delta gains up to 60 extra days a year above WBGT 32 °C, the level at which outdoor workers should limit heavy work to about 15 minutes in each hour. [VN-direct] Medium; projection; 2030 to 2040, 2050. CLM-16.
9. **Warming barely changes the fermentation cooling penalty, but a carbon price could.** We estimate that design wet-bulb temperature in the south rises 0.5 to 1.0 °C between 2026 and 2050. That adds only USD 1 to 6 per tonne of dry biomass to edition 1.0's cooling penalty of USD 70 to 180 per tonne. A carbon price on the extra cooling power, at today's grid factor, would add USD 13 to 26 per tonne at USD 29 per tCO2e (2030) and USD 42 to 80 per tonne at USD 90 (2040), that is 15 to 20% and 45 to 60% of the penalty. Clean power, not warming, decides tropical cooling cost. [VN-direct] Low; estimate; 2030, 2040, 2050. CLM-16, CLM-18, COST-08, COST-50, COST-51.
10. **Design for 40 °C or hotter to stay chiller-free through 2050.** By 2050, processes run at about 37 °C will need trim chilling on more hours. Strains that grow at 40 to 45 °C should still manage with cooling-tower water alone. [VN-direct] Low; estimate; 2050. CLM-16, CLM-38, COST-50.
11. **Maize import risk grows sharply with warming.** For the four largest maize exporters (87% of world exports), a year with losses above 10% in all four at once is near zero today. It becomes a 7% chance at 2 °C of global warming and an 86% chance at 4 °C. Vietnam imports about three-quarters of its maize. [general] High; projection (published probabilities); 2 °C around the 2040s. CLM-24.
12. **Soy yields are projected to peak around mid-century, then fall.** The losses are largest in the United States and Brazil, Vietnam's suppliers. In Brazil, soy and maize shift south and production falls in the Matopiba frontier region. Several breadbaskets could see climate impacts emerge before 2040. [general] Medium; projection; 2040 to 2100. CLM-25, CLM-26.
13. **Fishmeal supply risk rises with El Niño.** Models project extreme El Niño events roughly doubling, from about one in 20 years to one in 10. IPCC AR6 judges El Niño-related rainfall variability very likely to be amplified in the second half of the century. Both point to more frequent disruption of Peru's anchoveta seasons. The model basis is contested. [general] Medium; projection; 2050 to 2100. CLM-27, CLM-28, CLM-29.
14. **Official national estimates project large domestic crop losses.** The 2022 NDC gives rice yields minus 8.8% by 2030 and minus 15.1% by 2050, and maize minus 18.7% and minus 32.9%. About 1.1 Mt of farmed aquatic output (USD 935 million) is at risk from flooding each year. [VN-direct] Medium (scenario not stated); projection; 2030, 2050. CLM-08.
15. **Pangasius farms sit in the flood path.** With 50 cm of sea-level rise, every 2009 striped catfish farm site faces 2 m flood levels. At 75 cm, salinity shortens the grow-out season in former Soc Trang (now Can Tho), former Ben Tre (now Vinh Long) and coastal former Tien Giang (now Dong Thap). [VN-direct] Medium; projection; conditional on sea-level rise, mostly after 2050. CLM-20.
16. **Salinity is moving coastal land from rice to shrimp.** Plans and farmers are both shifting coastal land from rice to shrimp-rice and permanent shrimp. This raises demand for aquafeed protein and reduces local rice by-products (broken rice, bran) that could feed fermentation. [VN-direct] Medium; trend; 2030 to 2050. CLM-21, CLM-40.
17. **Cassava's main climate-linked risk is disease, not heat.** Cassava copes with heat and drought better than maize. But cassava mosaic disease, in Vietnam since 2017, cuts root yields by 16 to 33% when infected cuttings are planted. A single variety, KU50, covers more than 1 million ha in Thailand and Vietnam. For fermentation carbon supply to 2040, disease and competition from E10 ethanol matter more than climate. [VN-direct] Medium; trend; 2030 to 2040. CLM-30, CLM-31.
18. **Livestock heat stress roughly triples under high emissions.** For outdoor animals, the high-emission path (SSP5-8.5) gives about three times as many extreme heat-stress days, and three times the share of animals affected, as the low path (SSP1-2.6) by 2050. Vietnam's pig and poultry growth will depend on closed, cooled houses, which favours integrators and raises power demand. [general] Medium; projection; 2050. CLM-22, CLM-23.
19. **The macro stakes are large.** The World Bank estimates climate change could cost Vietnam 12 to 14.5% of GDP a year by 2050, against 3.2% in 2020. [VN-direct] Medium; projection; 2050. CLM-18, CLM-19.
20. **The official climate scenario is being replaced.** The 2025 national climate scenario (fifth edition) was due in Q1 2026. It is the first to use SSPs, at 4.4 km resolution for cities, with district-level sea-level rise. We could not confirm that it has been published. Its numbers will replace the 2020 RCP-based figures used here. [VN-direct] Medium; signal; 2026. CLM-09, CLM-10, CLM-39, CLM-43.

### 1. Vietnam climate projections

**Official scenarios.** The latest published national scenario is the 2020 edition (RCP-based). The 2022 NDC quotes it [CLM-08] {VN-direct|High}:

| Mekong Delta at risk of permanent flooding | | 47.3% of area at +100 cm | conditional | projection |
| Agricultural land at risk (national) | | 32.2% at +100 cm | conditional | projection |

- The 2025 update (fifth edition) was consulted on 2 July 2025. It uses SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0 and global warming levels of 1.5 to 4 °C. It has 4.4 km resolution for Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City and probabilistic sea-level rise (P5, P50, P95) by district. Release was expected in Q1 2026 [CLM-09; CLM-10; CLM-39; CLM-43] {VN-direct|Medium}. One preliminary number appeared in the press: +2 to 3.7 °C by 2100 under SSP3-7.0 (UK Met Office regional runs) [CLM-11] {VN-direct|Low}. We found no evidence of final publication by September 2026.
- IPCC AR6 global mean sea-level rise to 2100, relative to 1995 to 2014 (likely range): 0.32 to 0.62 m (SSP1-2.6), 0.44 to 0.76 m (SSP2-4.5), 0.63 to 1.01 m (SSP5-8.5) [CLM-28] {general|High}.

**Delta elevation and subsidence.**
- A new elevation model puts the Mekong Delta's mean elevation at about 0.8 m above local sea level, not the 2.6 m implied by global elevation data [CLM-12] {VN-direct|High}. Impact studies built on global data understate exposure.
- Groundwater-driven subsidence averaged 1.1 cm a year around 2015, and exceeded 2.5 cm in places. The delta sank about 18 cm in 25 years [CLM-13] {VN-direct|High}; trend.
- Projections of pumping-induced subsidence from 2018 to 2100 [CLM-14] {VN-direct|High}; projection:

| Groundwater pathway | Cumulative subsidence 2018 to 2100 |
|---|---|
| B2: extraction keeps growing | 100 cm (86 to 110) |
| B1: slower growth | 68 cm (55 to 70) |
| M1 to M4: mitigation | 39, 25, 19 and 12 cm |

- The low south-west of the delta (Ca Mau peninsula; now Ca Mau and An Giang provinces) falls below sea level this century in every pathway [CLM-14] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Reading.** To 2050, the choice of groundwater policy moves delta elevation more than the choice of global emissions pathway. At about 0.9 cm a year (B1 rate around 2023), pumping alone takes roughly 28 cm off the delta between 2018 and 2050. That is comparable to or larger than likely global sea-level rise over the same period (AR6 2050 values were not retrieved this wave). {VN-direct|Medium}; estimate; 2050.

**Salinity intrusion.**
- Over roughly the next three decades (to about 2050), subsidence and riverbed incision enlarge the salinity-affected area by 10 to 27%, and sea-level rise adds 6 to 19%. Climate change alone raises salinity by 2 to 2.5 ppt in coastal provinces by 2040. Riverbed incision runs at 200 to 300 mm a year, and upstream hydropower raises dry-season flow, partly offsetting sea-level rise inside the estuaries [CLM-15] {VN-direct|Medium}; projection.
- The 2019 to 2020 event affected 1,688,600 ha (42.5% of the delta). Losses: 26,000 ha of winter-spring rice lost outright, 8,715 ha of aquaculture damaged, 96,000 households (430,000 people) short of water [CLM-37] {VN-direct|Medium}; trend.
- In 2023 to 2024, the salinity front reached less far than in 2016 and 2020. About 1,580 ha of rice was at risk and 73,900 households were short of water. Causes cited: 128 upstream dams (231 planned by 2040 to 2060), drought, subsidence [CLM-36] {VN-direct|Medium}; signal.

**Heat and humid heat.**
- 68 stations, 1979 to 2018: daily maximum wet-bulb temperature rose at most stations. Heat-stress days rose by 0.8 to 4.2 days per decade (interquartile range) [CLM-17] {VN-direct|Medium}; trend.
- Downscaled CMIP6 projections (two global models, RegCM4 regional model, physically based WBGT) [CLM-16] {VN-direct|Medium}; projection:
  - Trend in annual maximum WBGT, 1985 to 2100: +0.2 °C per decade (SSP1-2.6), +0.3 (SSP2-4.5), +0.4 (SSP5-8.5).
  - Extra days a year above WBGT 32 °C in the Mekong Delta: under 60 in 2021 to 2040; 90 to 120 (SSP2-4.5) and over 150 (SSP5-8.5) in 2081 to 2100. The delta's rise exceeds 15 to 20 days per decade under SSP5-8.5, the steepest in Vietnam.
  - Results at 1.5 °C and 2 °C of global warming look like mid-century SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5.

- **Adaptation options named in the sources:** salinity-tolerant pangasius strains (least disruptive), higher dikes, shrimp-rice rotation. Recirculating systems and relocation upstream are discussed in the wider literature but we did not quantify them this wave.
- **What this means for protein supply (our reading).** Climate adaptation in the delta tends to *raise* demand for feed protein. Brackish shrimp replaces rice, and shrimp feed is protein-dense and uses fishmeal. It also removes some rice by-products that are candidate fermentation feedstocks. {VN-direct|Medium}; estimate; 2030 to 2050.
- **Gap.** We found no peer-reviewed, dated (2030 or 2050) projection of Vietnamese shrimp or pangasius output under SSP scenarios. Heat and disease links (for example, higher water temperature and shrimp disease outbreaks) are well known in practice but were not quantified here.

### 3. Livestock impacts

- For outdoor animals, extreme heat stress (THI-based) rises for all species. By 2050, the proportion of animals affected and the number of extreme days are about three times higher under SSP5-8.5 than under SSP1-2.6. "Poultry and pigs face major heat stress challenges in many parts of the tropics where they are currently raised." Global cattle example: 8 extreme days a year in 2000; 19 (SSP1-2.6) and 24 (SSP5-8.5) by 2050 [CLM-22] {general|Medium}; projection; 2050.
- In pigs, heat cuts feed intake, growth, lean deposition, sow reproduction and barn throughput. Feed efficiency falls above about 30 °C in finishing pigs. Changing the environment (cooling) is the most effective mitigation [CLM-23] {general|Medium}.
- The 2022 NDC expects pig headcount to fall about 8.2% under climate change (no year or scenario in the sentence) [CLM-08] {VN-direct|Low}.
- **Reading.** Vietnam's pig and poultry growth to 2050 will run through closed, evaporatively cooled houses. That raises power demand and capital per animal and favours large integrators over household farms. It also makes livestock supply more sensitive to electricity prices and outages. {VN-direct|Low}; estimate; 2030 to 2050.
- **Disease range shifts:** not researched this wave (see gaps).

Plants that design for more than one carbon source (cassava pulp, molasses, rice by-products, and later CO2 or methanol) reduce exposure. {VN-direct|Low}; estimate; 2030 to 2040.

**Numbers behind the caps (our calculations).**
- Agriculture emitted 104.5 MtCO2e in 2020 against a 2030 BAU of 112.1 [CLM-08]. The 64 MtCO2e cap is 38.8% below 2020; the 56 MtCO2e cap for 2050 is 46.4% below 2020.
- Implied 2020 methane: about 111 MtCO2e nationally and about 65.6 MtCO2e from agriculture (caps divided by 0.7).
- Livestock methane trajectory. A 2021 GIZ sectoral report gives livestock emissions (enteric plus manure, including N2O) of 27.2 MtCO2e in 2014, with BAU of 30.9 (2020) and 36.3 (2030) [CLM-34] {VN-direct|Medium}. Its methane part in 2014 was about 19.1 MtCO2e. Scaled along the BAU, livestock methane is about 21.7 MtCO2e in 2020 and 25.4 in 2030. The 15.2 cap is therefore about 30% below 2020 and 40% below 2030 BAU. {VN-direct|Low}; estimate; 2030.
- At 6.0 to 6.5 Mt of carcass meat in 2030 (Decision 1520 target, REG-54), methane per tonne of meat must fall from about 3.8 to about 2.3 to 2.5 tCO2e, a 34 to 39% cut in about five years. {VN-direct|Low}; estimate; 2030. Pork is about 60% of meat and pigs emit little enteric methane, so most of the cut must come from manure (biogas, covered storage, separation).

**What the caps imply for protein supply choices.**
- Ruminant expansion (beef, dairy) is the most methane-intensive growth path and sits awkwardly with a falling livestock cap. Growth in poultry, pigs with manure capture, and aquaculture is easier to reconcile. {VN-direct|Medium}; estimate; 2030 to 2050.
- Imported soy and maize carry land-use-change emissions abroad that Vietnam's inventory does not count. Domestic feed protein made with clean power could matter for exporters facing buyer or border rules (EU deforestation regulation for soy and cattle; possible future CBAM-type measures). This is a trade argument, not an inventory one. {general|Low}; signal; 2030 to 2040.
- A claim in MAE-affiliated media that agriculture "achieved and somewhat exceeded" its unconditional reduction targets is unverified. No post-2020 inventory was reachable [CLM-41] {VN-direct|Low}.

### 7. Water

- **Upstream dams.** With 7 Chinese mainstem dams and 133 proposed in the Lower Mekong, full build-out would trap about 96% of the sediment reaching the delta [CLM-32] {VN-adjacent|Medium}; projection. Press reports cite 128 dams by 2024 and 231 planned by 2040 to 2060 [CLM-36] {VN-direct|Low}. Dams also raise dry-season flow, which partly offsets salinity [CLM-15].
- **Groundwater.** Pumping drives subsidence (section 1). The Mekong plan restricts groundwater use in coastal and subsiding areas [CLM-21] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Fermentation siting (our estimate).** A 10,000 t a year aerobic plant using 20 to 40 m³ of water per tonne (edition 1.0) needs about 0.2 to 0.4 million m³ a year, or 550 to 1,100 m³ a day. That is small at basin scale but significant where groundwater is restricted. Delta sites should plan on surface water with dry-season salinity management. Sites in the Southeast (Ho Chi Minh City, Dong Nai, Tay Ninh) avoid delta subsidence, but their river intakes may face salinity in drought years; we did not verify this. {VN-direct|Low}; estimate; 2030 to 2050.

### 8. Warming and fermentation cooling

**Baseline (edition 1.0, unchanged).** Cooling towers in southern Vietnam deliver water at about 31 to 33 °C. That is too warm to hold a 28 to 32 °C broth, so most heat goes to chillers. The penalty is about 0.7 to 1.35 kWh per kg of dry biomass plus chiller capital: USD 70 to 180 per tonne, 2 to 4% of fungal feed-protein cost [COST-50; COST-51] {VN-direct|Low}. Vietnamese HVAC practice uses 37/32 °C cooling-water design and approach temperatures of 3 to 6 K above wet-bulb [CLM-38] {VN-direct|Low}. That is consistent with a design wet-bulb of about 27 to 28 °C, which remains unverified from a climatic-design dataset.

**Change to 2050 (our estimate).**

| Step | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Rise in design wet-bulb, 2026 to 2050 | +0.5 °C (SSP1-2.6) to +1.0 °C (SSP5-8.5); about +0.7 °C under SSP2-4.5 | WBGT maximum trend of 0.2 to 0.4 °C per decade [CLM-16] over 2.4 decades; WBGT used as a proxy for design wet-bulb |
| Rise relative to 1995 to 2014 | +0.9 to +1.8 °C by 2050 | Same trend over 4.5 decades |
| Extra chiller power | 2 to 4% more compressor power per K of condenser water (assumption) | Engineering rule of thumb, not sourced this wave |
| Extra cost at constant power price | USD 1 to 6 per t dry biomass (2026 to 2050); USD 1 to 11 per t relative to 1995 to 2014 | Our calculation on edition 1.0 lines |
| Carbon cost of the existing penalty at 0.66 tCO2/MWh | USD 13 to 26 per t at USD 29/tCO2e (15 to 20% of the penalty); USD 42 to 80 per t at USD 90/tCO2e (45 to 60%) | CCDR recommended path [CLM-18]; grid factor [COST-08] |
| Same at 0.1 tCO2/MWh (mostly renewable grid) | USD 2 to 12 per t | Our calculation |

**Reading.**
- Warming alone raises the tropical cooling penalty by only about 1 to 4% to 2050. That is well inside the uncertainty of the edition 1.0 estimate.
- The larger swing comes from the price and carbon content of power. A USD 90 carbon price on grid power without renewable supply would raise the effective penalty by about half. Clean power removes most of that.
- **Temperature thresholds.** On design days, tower water rises from about 31 to 33 °C today to about 31.5 to 34 °C by 2050. A 37 °C process (coolant needed at or below about 32 °C) moves from marginal to needing trim chillers on more hours. A 40 °C process (at or below about 35 °C) stays mostly on tower water. A 42 to 45 °C process stays chiller-free. {VN-direct|Low}; estimate; 2050.
- Heat stress on workers also matters for plant operations in the delta (section 1) but was not costed.

## Implications by audience

- **Investors.** Price climate as volatility in imported feed protein, not as a smooth trend. The maize-shock probabilities (7% at 2 °C) and a possible doubling of extreme El Niño support the thesis for domestic feed protein as insurance value, not as a price forecast. For fermentation, warming is a minor cost line; the grid factor and any carbon price are larger.
- **Policy makers.** To 2050, groundwater control is the highest-leverage delta policy, because subsidence outruns sea-level rise. The livestock methane cap (15.2 MtCO2e by 2030) needs a named delivery path (manure capture), or growth targets and caps will clash. Publish the post-2020 inventory and the 2025 climate scenario so that planners stop relying on 2016 and 2020 numbers.
- **Startups.** Choose strains that run at 40 °C or hotter; they stay chiller-free through 2050. Site in the Southeast or on stable ground, with surface water. Design for more than one carbon source.
- **Feed and food manufacturers.** Expect more frequent and more synchronised shocks in maize, soy and fishmeal. Salinity-driven growth in shrimp raises aquafeed protein demand in the delta. Heat pushes livestock into closed houses, which changes feed conversion and formulation.
- **Research bodies.** Priority gaps are dated, SSP-based projections for Vietnamese shrimp and pangasius, heat effects on closed-house pig and poultry performance in Vietnam, thermotolerant production strains, and CMD-resistant cassava.
- **International bodies.** Fundable public goods include groundwater monitoring and substitution in the delta, sediment management, salinity forecasting, and a transparent agricultural emissions inventory with livestock MRV (measurement, reporting and verification).
